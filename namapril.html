Task Response 4 The essay attempts to address both views, but the treatment of each is very thin and underdeveloped. The "education is effective" side mentions school and social media but offers no real argument or elaboration. The "education is not effective" side, which should explain why education fails, instead just describes why junk food is popular, which is a different point entirely. Crucially, the writer never clearly states or defends a personal opinion, which the task explicitly requires. The conclusion largely repeats the introduction rather than synthesising an argument. Read Bình's essays to learn how to properly develop relevant ideas for this task.
Coherence & Cohesion 4 There is a basic four-paragraph structure, which shows some awareness of organisation. However, progression within paragraphs breaks down quickly. Ideas are listed rather than developed, and logical connections between sentences are often missing or unclear. For example, the second body paragraph shifts from explaining why education fails to describing junk food's popularity, with no link between the two. The conclusion does not follow logically from the body. Basic connectors (however, while, therefore) are used, but they are sometimes misapplied.
Lexical resource 4 The vocabulary range is limited and repetitive: junk food, healthy food, people, and chooes appear constantly with little variation. There are persistent spelling errors throughout: belive (believe), socical (social), chooes (choose), quik (quick), attact (attract), rasie (raise), understrand (understand). Word choice is often imprecise or unnatural: "something is good for their health", "which is provide vitamins", "bring harmful", "benefits" used to describe junk food's appeal. These errors are frequent enough to cause the reader noticeable difficulty.
Grammatical range & accuracy 4 Simple sentences occasionally work, but attempts at more complex structures consistently produce errors. Key problems include: "they choices something" (should be they choose something), "which is provide" (incorrect auxiliary), "others people" (no plural on other), "It is convenient, quik and even tasty leads to" (sentence structure breaks down entirely), "it can bring harmful" (wrong word class; harm not harmful). Subordinate clauses are attempted but frequently malformed. Grammatical errors are frequent and sometimes prevent the intended meaning from coming through clearly.

Reasons for a low score
  • No personal opinion is stated or defended, which is a direct task requirement
  • Body paragraphs are short and under-developed
  • Spelling errors are more frequent and more disruptive to reading
  • The "against education" argument misses the point. It describes junk food's popularity rather than explaining why education would fail to change behaviour.
Top Priorities to Move Forward
  • Answer the full question. This task asks you to discuss both views AND give your opinion. You must clearly state which side you agree with and explain why, ideally in the introduction and reinforced in the conclusion.
  • Develop each paragraph around one clear idea. State the point → explain it → give an example or reason → link back. Your paragraphs currently state a point and then drift.
  • Proofread for spelling. Words like choose, believe, raise, and social appear misspelled multiple times. Practising these high-frequency words directly would noticeably improve your Lexical Resource score.

The moment the topic becomes abstract or unfamiliar, there is no vocabulary, no grammatical structure, and no communication strategy (paraphrase, buying time, rephrasing the question) to fall back on.

Areas of improvement
  • Final consonant sounds — dropping word endings (face, exercise, because) is a systemic phonological habit that affects intelligibility across the entire test. This needs dedicated pronunciation drilling, not just general speaking practice.
  • Part 3 survival strategies — learning phrases like "That's an interesting question — I think one factor might be..." or "I'm not sure exactly, but perhaps..." buys processing time and prevents a complete breakdown even when ideas are slow to come.
  • Basic sentence accuracy first — before targeting complex structures, consistent control of simple present, simple past, plurals, and articles would raise the grammatical score meaningfully.
  • Vocabulary building by topic — the IELTS speaking topics are predictable (media, education, technology, environment, society). Even learning 8–10 words per topic would significantly reduce the risk of another Part 3 collapse.
